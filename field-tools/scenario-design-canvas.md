# Field Tool 003 — Scenario Design Canvas

### A design tool for turning content into decisions under realistic conditions

> **Scenario is the product. Friction is the mechanism. Evidence is the output.**

A scenario is not a story wrapped around content.

It is a **decision environment**.

The Scenario Design Canvas helps designers move from *What should we teach?* to a more operational question:

> **What must the person notice, decide, and do when reality stops matching the clean version of the plan?**

The canvas is designed for operational learning, autonomous systems, technical training, public safety, high-consequence communication, and any environment where capability has to survive uncertainty.

## The Scenario Architecture

> # MISSION → SIGNAL → FRICTION → LIMIT → DECISION → CONSEQUENCE → EVIDENCE

### 1. MISSION
**What is the operator trying to accomplish?**

Define the operational objective rather than the learning objective.

The learner should enter the scenario with something meaningful to protect, achieve, restore, prevent, or complete.

### 2. SIGNAL
**What changes?**

Introduce the cue, anomaly, contradiction, event, or new information that makes routine execution insufficient.

A useful signal demands attention without automatically revealing the answer.

### 3. FRICTION
**What makes the decision difficult?**

Add realistic pressure:

- incomplete or conflicting information;
- time compression;
- degraded communications;
- automation behaving unexpectedly;
- competing priorities;
- resource constraints;
- ambiguity;
- distraction;
- uncertainty about authority or procedure.

Friction should expose judgment—not manufacture confusion for its own sake.

### 4. LIMIT
**What constrains the response?**

Define the boundaries that matter:

- safety;
- authority;
- policy;
- rules of engagement;
- system capability;
- available resources;
- time;
- environment;
- uncertainty.

A meaningful decision has boundaries.

### 5. DECISION
**What must the operator decide?**

Write the decision explicitly.

Avoid scenarios whose real task is simply remembering a fact.

Ask what action the operator must select, reject, delay, escalate, modify, or defend.

### 6. CONSEQUENCE
**What changes because of the decision?**

Let the scenario respond.

Consequences may affect:

- mission progress;
- safety;
- system state;
- available options;
- trust;
- time;
- information;
- another person or team;
- the next decision.

The consequence creates the next operating condition.

### 7. EVIDENCE
**What observable behavior demonstrates capability?**

Capture more than whether the learner reached the preferred outcome.

Look for evidence that the operator:

- noticed the relevant signal;
- interpreted the condition;
- recognized limits;
- made a justified decision;
- acted appropriately;
- explained the reasoning;
- updated when new evidence appeared.

## Scenario Quality Check

Before releasing the scenario, ask:

| Test | Question |
|---|---|
| **Mission** | Is there an operational objective rather than merely a learning objective? |
| **Signal** | Does something meaningful change? |
| **Friction** | Is judgment required under realistic pressure? |
| **Limits** | Are relevant boundaries present? |
| **Decision** | Is there a consequential choice rather than simple recall? |
| **Options** | Are multiple actions plausible enough to require discrimination? |
| **Consequence** | Does the environment respond to the decision? |
| **Evidence** | Can performance be observed rather than inferred from completion? |
| **Debrief** | Can the learner defend the reasoning and update from the outcome? |

## The Anti-Scenario Test

A scenario is probably still disguised content if:

- the answer is obvious from the wording;
- every relevant fact is conveniently available;
- there is no meaningful constraint;
- nothing changes after the decision;
- the learner cannot make a plausible mistake;
- success is measured only by selecting the expected answer;
- the scenario tests memory but claims to test judgment.

> **If removing the story leaves an ordinary multiple-choice question, you probably did not build a scenario.**

## Design for Branching Consequences

A useful scenario can operate as a loop:

> **SIGNAL → DECISION → CONSEQUENCE → NEW CONDITION → NEXT SIGNAL**

The purpose of branching is not to create more screens.

It is to make the learner experience the consequences of a decision and then make another decision from the changed state.

## Debrief for Judgment

Do not end with only **Correct** or **Incorrect**.

Ask:

**What did you notice?**  
**What did you believe was true?**  
**What constraint mattered most?**  
**What option did you reject—and why?**  
**What evidence supported your decision?**  
**What new information would have changed it?**  
**What will you carry into the next situation?**

The debrief turns experience into an update.

## The Standard

A scenario succeeds when it makes judgment visible.

It should create a realistic reason to notice, interpret, choose, act, and explain—not merely another way to deliver information.

> **Scenario is the product.**
